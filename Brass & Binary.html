The job

Three missions, one officer.

A Cyber Warfare Officer plans, leads and executes cyberspace operations at Army and joint levels, and commands the teams that carry them out.

OCO

Offensive operations

Create effects against adversary systems and networks when a commander or the nation needs them, as part of a larger operation.

DCO

Defensive operations

Hunt for threats inside the networks the Army and the joint force rely on, and remove them before they cost a mission.

DODIN

Network operations

Build, run and secure the Department of Defense Information Network that every other operation depends on.

The branch

Army Cyber

The Army created its Cyber Branch in 2014, the first new basic branch since Special Forces in 1987. Today it fields officers, warrant officers, enlisted soldiers and civilians who defend Army networks and conduct cyberspace operations for the joint force.

What a 17A does

Lead the operation, command the team.

On the job

  • Plan, integrate, synchronize and execute defensive and offensive cyberspace operations and DODIN operations at Army and joint, interagency and multinational levels.
  • Command, lead and manage Cyber Mission Force teams and the units around them.
  • Understand friendly and adversary cyberspace capabilities, objectives and organizations, and brief commanders who may never have taken a cybersecurity course.
  • Early in a career, lead a team of soldiers and Army civilians focused on one cyberspace mission, such as network defense or intelligence-driven targeting.

Your tools of the trade

  • Army and joint doctrine, policy and legal authorities for cyberspace operations.
  • Cyber and electronic warfare threat knowledge, and the electromagnetic spectrum fundamentals behind it.
  • Planning and synchronization skills that fold cyber effects into a commander's wider plan.
  • Network, antenna, electro-optic and space-based capability fundamentals taught at the Cyber School.

Where the work happens

The Cyber Mission Force.

U.S. Cyber Command's operational arm is the Cyber Mission Force, built from 133 teams supplied by all the services. Army cyber officers lead many of them.

Cyber National Mission Force

Teams that defend the nation against significant cyber threats and conduct operations under national direction.

Cyber Protection Force

Cyber Protection Teams that defend the Department of Defense's networks and key systems.

Cyber Combat Mission Force

Teams that support combatant commanders with cyber effects for their operations.

Cyber has its own selection board

Commissioning does not automatically put you in Cyber. The Cyber School runs a Cyber Branch Assessment and Selection Process with an interview, an aptitude assessment, a written questionnaire and a review of your supporting packet. Direct commissioning, covered on the Join page, is the one route that places you in Cyber from the start.

The chain of command

Two commands, one fight.

U.S. Cyber Command sets the joint mission. Army Cyber Command provides the Army's forces and runs the Army's cyberspace operations. As a 17A you would serve in or alongside both.

Army component

Army Cyber Command (ARCYBER)

The Army's operational cyber headquarters. It directs and conducts cyberspace operations and is also designated the Army's Joint Force Headquarters-Cyber, which supports U.S. Cyber Command.

The Army chose Fort Gordon in 2013 so that its operational headquarters would sit beside the Cyber School, NSA-Georgia and most of its cyber mission forces. Leaders describe the current focus as setting the land domain through cyber, defending the homeland and adopting AI-enabled operations.

The unified combatant command that conducts offensive and defensive cyberspace operations for the nation and the joint force. It shares Fort Meade with the National Security Agency.

Where you will live

Three posts that matter.

Cyber officers train and serve at two main hubs, with a detour through Fort Benning for Officer Candidate School. In the first Cyber BOLC class, 11 graduates went to Fort Gordon, nine to Fort Meade and one to Korea.

Train and serve

Fort Gordon, Georgia

Next to Augusta and roughly a two-and-a-half-hour drive from Atlanta. It is the home of Army cyber training and of Army Cyber Command headquarters.

  • Army Cyber Center of Excellence and the Army Cyber School, where you take Cyber BOLC.
  • Army Cyber Command headquarters and Army JFHQ-Cyber.
  • NSA-Georgia, a major signals intelligence and cyber site beside the Army's cyber mission forces.
  • The Signal School, which shares the campus and classrooms.

Lower local housing costs than the Washington region mean a housing allowance goes further here.

Name history: Fort Gordon became Fort Eisenhower in 2023 and returned to Fort Gordon on June 10, 2025. It now honors Master Sgt. Gary Gordon, a Medal of Honor recipient from the Battle of Mogadishu.

Serve

Fort George G. Meade, Maryland

Between Baltimore and Washington, D.C. It is the center of gravity for joint and national cyber operations.

  • U.S. Cyber Command headquarters.
  • The National Security Agency, with Cyber Command's operations integrated beside it.
  • The Cyber National Mission Force and the DoD Cyber Defense Command.
  • An ARCYBER liaison element working directly with CYBERCOM.

A new NSA and CYBERCOM East Campus is scheduled for completion in 2028 and is planned to house more than 20,000 personnel. Housing costs are higher here, and so is the housing allowance.

Commissioning

Fort Benning, Georgia

Home of Army Officer Candidate School, a 12-week course at the Maneuver Center of Excellence in Columbus. The post was named Fort Moore from 2023 until April 2025 and again honors Cpl. Fred G. Benning, a World War I Distinguished Service Cross recipient. You only pass through here if you commission through OCS.

The process

How to become a Cyber Warfare Officer

There is no single door. You can commission through West Point, ROTC, Officer Candidate School or a direct commission, then branch into Cyber and train at Fort Gordon. This page walks the most common route for a civilian with a degree.

Before you apply

Are you eligible?

The baseline

  • U.S. citizen. The Cyber School lists citizenship as a requirement, and the Top Secret clearance needs it.
  • Bachelor's degree to attend federal OCS. ROTC and West Point build the degree into the path.
  • ASVAB GT score of 110 or higher for OCS. Retesting is allowed.
  • Age: federal OCS applicants generally need to commission before their 34th birthday. Waivers exist.
  • Medically, physically and morally qualified. You will take the Army fitness test.

The cyber layer

  • Clearance. Eligible for Secret to start, then a Top Secret/SCI investigation begins when you are branched Cyber.
  • Cyber Branch selection. Interview, aptitude assessment, written questionnaire and a packet review.
  • Technical credibility. A degree in computing, engineering or math helps. Certifications, internships, competitions and real work experience strengthen a packet.
  • Leadership record. Boards look for people who can lead soldiers, not only operate tools.

Step by step

The OCS pipeline.

  1. 1

    Talk to an Army officer recruiter

    Ask about OCS, Cyber and direct commissioning. A recruiter builds your packet, schedules testing and walks you through the board. Bring your transcripts and any certifications.

    Start here
  2. 2

    Take the ASVAB

    The Armed Services Vocational Aptitude Battery measures what you can learn. For officers the number that matters is the GT (General Technical) line score, built from the verbal and arithmetic reasoning sections. OCS requires a 110 or higher. If you miss it you can retest. Officer candidates who need a score can also take the Armed Forces Classification Test.

    GT 110 or higherRetest allowed
  3. 3

    Clear medical and fitness

    You complete a medical screening at a Military Entrance Processing Station and must meet height, weight and Army fitness standards. Start training early. OCS is a physical course.

    MEPS physicalArmy fitness test
  4. 4

    Compete for selection

    Your packet goes to an OCS selection board that weighs your degree, test scores, leadership record, character and interview. Separately, the Cyber School runs its own assessment and selection for the Cyber Branch.

    OCS boardCyber Branch assessment
  5. 5

    Basic Combat Training

    If you have no prior service you first enlist under an officer candidate option and complete about 10 weeks of Basic Combat Training before OCS.

    About 10 weeks
  6. 6

    Officer Candidate School

    Twelve weeks at Fort Benning. The first half builds leadership fundamentals, and the second half moves to field exercises where you lead others under evaluation. Candidates are paid at roughly an E-5 rate. You graduate and commission as a second lieutenant.

    12 weeksFort Benning, GA
  7. 7

    Clearance investigation

    When you are branched Cyber you start a Top Secret/SCI investigation. Investigators look at finances, foreign contacts, drug use, conduct and honesty. Some assignments also need a polygraph. Keep your record clean from today.

    TS/SCI
  8. 8

    Cyber BOLC at Fort Gordon

    The Cyber Warfare Officer Basic Officer Leader Course, often called CyBOLC, runs roughly six to nine months. You study doctrine, policy and authorities, cyber and electronic warfare threats, planning and synchronization, the electromagnetic spectrum, networks and signals intelligence support. You graduate qualified as a 17A.

    6–9 monthsFort Gordon, GA
  9. 9

    Your first Cyber Mission Force team

    You report to a team at Fort Gordon, Fort Meade or another cyber unit. Around your fourth year you attend the Captains Career Course at the Cyber School, and you begin leading larger groups.

    First assignment

Direct commissioning

The Army Talent Innovation Division brings in civilian technologists as officers. Cyber candidates should show proven leadership in tactical or technical settings, strong communication and at least eight years of cyber-related experience. In 2026 the Army said it is expanding direct commissions in cyber, AI and space at the lieutenant and captain level. Historically the program has commissioned fewer than 50 officers a year, mostly in medicine, law and the chaplaincy, so treat it as selective.

Service obligation

How long you owe depends on your commissioning source and the training you receive. ROTC scholarship cadets owe four years of active duty. Government auditors have noted that Army guidance on obligations for advanced cyber training has been unclear. Ask your recruiter to put your exact obligation in writing before you sign.

Beyond the paycheck

The benefits stack.

+

Health care

TRICARE Prime has no enrollment fee, deductible or copays for active-duty members, and military pharmacies fill prescriptions at no cost. Family members on Prime also pay nothing for covered care. Dental is included for you, and families use a separate dental plan.

+

Housing

The Basic Allowance for Housing is tax-free and set to cover about 95% of typical local housing costs. Rate protection keeps it from dropping while you stay at the same post. You can also choose on-post housing.

+

Food

The Basic Allowance for Subsistence is a flat, tax-free monthly payment: $328.48 for officers in 2026. It does not vary by location or rank.

+

The Exchange and commissary

The Exchange sells everyday goods and electronics without sales tax. Commissaries sell groceries at close to cost. Both are open to active-duty members and families on and near most posts.

+

Retirement

Under the Blended Retirement System you earn a pension after 20 years, 40% of your highest 36-month average base pay for life. You also get a Thrift Savings Plan with 1% automatic contributions and up to 4% matched, so up to 5% from the government.

+

Education

Tuition assistance helps pay for courses while you serve. The Post-9/11 GI Bill covers up to 36 months of school, housing and books, and eligible members can transfer it to family. Army recruiting advertises up to 46 nationally recognized certifications across cyber roles.

+

Time off

Thirty days of paid leave a year, earned at 2.5 days a month, plus federal holidays that are normally non-duty days.

+

Home and family

VA-backed home loans allow no down payment for eligible borrowers. Low-cost Servicemembers' Group Life Insurance is available, and moves between posts are paid for by the Army.

+

Retention pay

The Army has offered retention bonuses to cyber officers in the past because private-sector demand for the same skills is high. Amounts change and are not guaranteed.

What you take with you

The exit ramp is wide.

A clearance

A Top Secret/SCI clearance is valuable to defense contractors, intelligence agencies and security firms that need cleared talent.

Leadership history

Leading a team under pressure at 23 is a résumé line most civilian cyber professionals do not have until much later.

Education benefits

The GI Bill can fund a graduate degree. Veterans also receive preference in many federal hiring decisions.

Certifications

Army training and credentialing programs can add industry certifications that private employers recognize.

The honest part

What it costs.

A fair decision needs both columns. These are the trade-offs most officers mention.

Lifestyle

  • You move to a new post every few years, and the Army has a large say in where.
  • You cannot quit midstream. A service obligation binds you for the length of your contract.
  • Cyber threats do not follow a duty schedule, so some weeks run long.
  • Deployments and temporary duty are part of the job.

Career

  • Top private-sector cyber roles can pay more in cash than a mid-career officer earns.
  • You work under military law, a chain of command and fitness standards.
  • The clearance process is intrusive, and some assignments require a polygraph.
  • Commissioning does not guarantee Cyber, and Cyber does not guarantee your preferred post.

If the trade sounds right, start the path.

The next step is a conversation with an Army officer recruiter. Bring questions about service obligations, branching and assignments, and ask for answers in writing.
